RX 01

The Fit Question

Neither job posting looks like George's résumé at first glance. Nearly ten years of his career sits inside a high-volume, centralized, and telehealth model — verifying scripts and running MTM calls from a desk, not standing at a compounding hood or walking a facility floor. That's the honest gap, and it shows up differently for each role:

RoleWhat's genuinely newWhat already transfers
Compounding / BHRT
Debary, FL		 Hands-on sterile & non-sterile compounding; USP 795/797/800 execution Drug utilization review, dosing/interaction scrutiny, regulatory rigor, patient education on hormone-adjacent chronic conditions
LTC Pharmacist
SpecialtyRx		 Facility/nursing-staff coordination; on-call duties; geriatric population in a residential setting Order evaluation for drug/dose/interaction appropriateness, therapy-change recommendations to prescribers, controlled-substance and documentation discipline, geriatric pharmacotherapy as a named core qualification
The bridge, in one line: he isn't asking either employer to take a chance on unproven clinical judgment — that part is already documented at volume. What's new is the setting it gets applied in.

Bridge script

If asked directly about the gap"I'll be straight with you — I haven't stood at a compounding hood / worked inside a facility day-to-day. What I have done for almost ten years is the part that's hardest to teach: catching the interaction, the wrong dose, the therapy that doesn't fit the patient, before it becomes a problem, and doing it at a volume most pharmacists never see. [500+ scripts/day, 90-100 calls/day]. I'm confident the clinical judgment transfers — I'm here because I want to apply it somewhere [more hands-on / more embedded with a care team]."

RX 03

Explaining His Background, Plainly

Interviewers outside the chain-pharmacy world won't automatically know what "central processing" or "OutcomesMTM" means. Translate, don't assume.

  • Central Processing Operations → "A high-volume verification hub — every prescription from a large group of stores gets checked here for safety before it's filled. I'm the last clinical check before it reaches the patient."
  • Clinical Outbound Pharmacist / OutcomesMTM → "I run structured medication reviews by phone — going through everything a patient takes, catching gaps or risks, and coordinating with their doctor when something needs to change."
  • Pharmacist Chat Team → "Real-time video and chat consultations — same clinical judgment as an in-person counseling window, just delivered remotely."

Fallback line if the interviewer pushes on the gap

Use if pressed twice on the same gap"I won't pretend the setting is the same — but the muscle is. Every one of those [500+] daily verifications is a judgment call about whether a therapy is safe and appropriate for that specific patient. That's the same judgment call this role needs, just applied where I can see it through in person."

STAR Story Bank

Each story below is mapped to duties pulled directly from the postings. BOTH stories are the highest-leverage — lead with those first.

1. Catching a dangerous interaction at volume

BOTH — DUR / order evaluation

S: During a routine shift verifying [500+] prescriptions. T: Identify a [drug interaction / contraindication / dosing error] before it reached the patient. A: Flagged it, contacted the prescriber directly, proposed [alternative therapy or dose adjustment]. R: [Prevented adverse event / therapy corrected same day] — documented per protocol.

2. Recommending a therapy change that stuck

LTC — recommend therapy changes to physician/facility staff

S: Comprehensive Medication Review flagged a patient on [medication/condition, e.g. a diabetic patient with an adherence gap]. T: Get a practical, evidence-based recommendation in front of the prescriber. A: Documented the clinical rationale, called the prescriber, proposed [specific change]. R: Change adopted; [adherence or outcome improved — pull from his 150+ CMR/wk track record].

RX 05

Toughest Likely Questions

Built around the fit gap

  • "You've spent your whole career centralized or remote — what makes you think you'll like being on your feet in a facility / at a hood?" (Have a genuine, specific answer ready — not just "I'm ready for a change.")
  • "Walk me through your compounding experience." (For Job 1 — don't overstate; pivot immediately to DUR/safety rigor and eagerness to be trained.)
  • "How would you handle a 2 a.m. on-call from a facility nurse about a resident's medication?" (For Job 2 — draw on his chat-team experience triaging urgent concerns remotely.)
  • "You're used to a script telling you what to check. Facility/BHRT work is less structured — how do you handle ambiguity?"
  • "Why now, after ten years in one system?"

Standard but expect them

  • Tell me about a time you disagreed with a prescriber.
  • How do you prioritize when everything feels urgent?
  • What does patient-centered care mean to you, day to day?
RX 06

Why Him

The honest, defensible case — not oversold:

  • Volume nobody else brings. 500+ verifications a day and 150+ Comprehensive Medication Reviews a week isn't a claim, it's a documented run rate. Most candidates for these roles haven't seen this many patient profiles.
  • Geriatric and chronic disease pharmacotherapy is a named core qualification, not an inferred skill — directly relevant to an LTC population and to hormone-adjacent chronic condition management in BHRT.
  • Communication under resistance is already proven. 90-100 outbound calls a day means he's spent a year getting hesitant, skeptical, or frustrated people to engage — the same skill a facility nurse or a new BHRT patient will need from him.
  • Regulatory instinct is built in, not bolted on. HIPAA, controlled substances, federal/state pharmacy law — he's operated inside strict frameworks for a decade. USP 795/797/800 is a new rulebook, not a new discipline.
  • He's shown he can re-platform fast. Two internal role changes at Walgreens in four years, each requiring new systems and a new care model, both landed successfully.

RX 08

Day-Of Cheat Sheet

Before you walk in

  • Arrive 10-15 minutes early — no earlier. Use the extra minutes in the car, not the lobby, to run the 60-second opening once out loud.
  • Bring: 2 printed copies of the résumé, a copy of the active FL pharmacist license, a notepad and pen, and the 2 questions for the interviewer written down so nerves can't erase them.
  • Phone fully silent, not just on vibrate — set it before getting out of the car.
  • If it's the Debary compounding role: expect a working pharmacy environment — closed-toe shoes, hair tied back, minimal jewelry, no strong fragrance.
  • If it's the SpecialtyRx LTC role: expect it may be a straightforward office/facility interview — standard professional dress is enough.

Numbers to have cold

Say these out loud twice before going in — under pressure, round numbers are what disappear first.
  • 500+ prescriptions verified per day
  • 90–100 outbound clinical calls per day
  • 150+ Comprehensive Medication Reviews per week
  • ~40 Targeted Medication Reviews per week
  • Nearly 10 years at Walgreens, three roles: central processing (2016) → chat/video team (2022) → outbound clinical (2025)
  • PharmD, University of Florida, May 2014

If it turns into the Job 1 conversation (Compounding / BHRT)

  • Open the gap yourself before they have to ask — see the Rx 01 bridge script.
  • Lead with USP-adjacent language: "regulatory rigor," "attention to detail," "willingness to be trained on the technical side."
  • Don't guess at compounding specifics he doesn't know — say plainly he's new to sterile/non-sterile technique and ask what the training path looks like.

If it turns into the Job 2 conversation (LTC / SpecialtyRx)

  • Lead with the DUR/order-evaluation overlap — this is his strongest direct match of the two roles.
  • Have a real answer ready for the on-call question (see Rx 05) — draw on the chat-team triage experience.
  • If asked about facility/nursing coordination specifically, be honest it's new, then pivot to prescriber-communication reps from daily verification work.

Lead with

  • Judgment, verification, safety check
  • Patient-centered, adherence, barrier
  • Documented, evidence-based
  • Ready to learn, coachable
  • Volume, consistency, track record

Avoid

  • "Just verifying scripts" (undersells the clinical judgment)
  • "I've never done that" (bridge instead — see Rx 01)
  • Over-apologizing for the gap
  • Chain-pharmacy jargon without translation
  • Vague claims — always land on a number or outcome

Questions to ask the interviewer

Pick 2-3 based on which role this is. These are built to sound like someone thinking about how to actually do the job well — not filler questions.

If it's the Compounding / BHRT role (Debary)

  • "What's a typical shift's compounding volume look like — roughly how many BHRT preparations versus weight-loss preparations, and how does that split between sterile and non-sterile?"
  • "How is the cleanroom set up — how many hoods, and is non-sterile compounding physically separated from sterile?"
  • "For someone strong on the clinical/verification side but newer to hands-on compounding, what does the onboarding ramp look like before I'd be compounding independently?"
  • "Is there a second-pharmacist check on every batch, or is verification split by volume?"
  • "You mentioned expanding operations — is this part-time schedule expected to grow toward full-time, and on what kind of timeline has that happened for others?"

If it's the LTC role (SpecialtyRx)

  • "Walk me through what happens between a facility submitting an order and it going out the door — how many hands does it pass through, and where does a pharmacist's review sit in that flow?"
  • "How many facilities would I be supporting, and is coverage assigned by facility or by task across the pharmacist team?"
  • "When a therapy-change recommendation goes to a prescriber, what's the typical turnaround, and how is that followed up on if it goes quiet?"
  • "How often does on-call actually get activated, and what does a typical on-call call look like — urgent clinical questions, or more logistics?"
  • "What system handles order processing and documentation, and how much of the day is spent in that system versus on the phone with nursing staff?"

Works for either

  • "What's the biggest workflow or safety bottleneck the team is dealing with right now that this hire is meant to help with?"
  • "What does success in the first 90 days actually look like, day to day — not the title, the tasks?"

If nerves spike mid-answer

Reset, don't rush"That's a good question — give me one second to think about the clearest example." A three-second pause reads as composure, not hesitation. Then go straight to the closest STAR story from Rx 04.

Right after the interview

  • Send a same-day thank-you note referencing one specific thing discussed — not a generic template.
  • Note anything asked that wasn't covered here, so the guide can be sharpened for the next round.
